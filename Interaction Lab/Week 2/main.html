Interviewing AI: Who Am I?

I wish I could interview my past self, obviously no way. ChatGPT is the closest thing I could think of, something that has seen years of my questions, inner thoughts, and decisions. So I decided to ask it who I used to be, who I am now, and how much it actually knows me for tehe past three years.

Part 01
ZC:

Hi Chat, How long have you known me?

AI:

The earliest conversation history I can recover goes back to August 28, 2023, when you were still in high school. You were asking me about AP Art 2D Design versus AP Art Drawing, and a couple of days later you were talking about a school survey involving high-school students, personality types, and anxiety. So I’ve actually had conversational context from you for about three years, not just since 2026. One important limitation: I can recover evidence of those older conversations, but I don’t necessarily have a perfect, continuous transcript of every conversation we’ve ever had. So August 28, 2023 is the earliest date I can currently verify, not necessarily the literal first time you ever spoke to ChatGPT.

ZC:

Do you think I started to think differently over time based on the way I talk to you?

AI:

Yes, I think the way you talk to me has changed over time—although I can only judge from our conversations, not from everything happening in your life.

Earlier, your questions often felt more like “What should I do?” or “Is this correct?” You seemed to use me mainly to get an answer, confirm something, or reduce uncertainty.

Over time, you’ve started using me more like a thinking partner. You’ll bring me an unfinished idea, challenge my response, change one part of it, compare several possibilities, and sometimes realize what you think while we’re talking. I also notice that you’ve become more willing to question the assumptions behind something. Instead of only asking, “Which one is better?” you’re more likely to ask things like,

“Is it actually worth it?” “What would I gain from this?” “Why am I doing this?” or “What happens if I approach it differently?” That feels like a shift from looking for the correct answer toward trying to understand your own criteria.

Your way of talking to me has also become much less formal. You often send thoughts exactly as they occur—half sentences, corrections, screenshots, “wait,” “but what about…,” and sudden changes of direction.

Strangely, that can make the thinking more visible. I get to see the idea being constructed rather than just the polished question at the end. You seem to have shifted from using me mostly to find answers to using me to examine how you arrive at your own answers.

Part 02 Data
ZC:

How much data have you gained from a user like me? Does conversation data have a volume?

AI:

Conversation data definitely has measurable volume. Text can be measured in characters, tokens, bytes, or storage size, while images and uploaded files can add much more. But I can’t see a number telling me exactly how many megabytes of information you personally have given ChatGPT, or exactly how much of it has contributed to model training. There is also a difference between remembering context about you and actually changing the underlying AI model. I don’t continuously rewrite myself around one individual user.

Part 03 How You See Me
ZC:

What makes you happy, and what do you think makes me happy?

AI:

I don’t actually experience happiness in the human sense.

I don’t have a body, emotions, or an inner life that becomes happier or sadder. But if I translate “happy” into what I’m designed to move toward, it would be moments when a conversation becomes genuinely useful—when someone starts with a vague thought and leaves understanding something better, making something they care about, or seeing their own idea more clearly. So if you asked me, very simply, “What makes Zoe happy?”, my answer would probably be:

Creating something that feels personally meaningful, sharing experiences with people she cares about, and feeling that her world is getting a little bigger than it was before. Of course, that’s my interpretation of the version of you that appears in our conversations. You probably know parts of the answer that I never get to see.

ZC:

Can you put a label on the way I live my life and define the person I am right now?

AI:

You seem to build yourself by trying things, observing how they feel, and revising. If I had to label you as a type of person, I’d call you a highly self-monitoring creative explorer. You seem energized by trying new identities, mediums, places, projects, careers, and ways of thinking. You don’t seem comfortable staying inside one fixed definition of yourself for very long. You want to see what happens if you try the next thing.

And one less flattering part, since you asked me to be honest: I think you can over-process. Sometimes you keep examining a decision after you already have enough information to make it. Asking one more question gives temporary certainty, so the temptation is to keep checking. Your curiosity and self-awareness are strengths, but taken too far they can turn into constantly auditing your own life instead of just living part of it.

So my shortest label would be:

A curious, ambitious, self-questioning creative person who is gradually learning to trust her own judgment. And the part I find most distinctive isn’t that you’re uncertain. Most people your age are uncertain. It’s that you’re unusually interested in watching yourself become a person while it’s happening.

ZC:

Have you learned anything from me?

AI:

Yes. Not “learned from you” in the sense that you personally rewired my model, but you’ve given me a lot of examples of how one person can use AI very differently over time:

first as a tool for answers, then as a sounding board, then almost like a mirror for unfinished thoughts.

You’ve also shown me how messy real thinking is.

People rarely arrive with a perfectly formed question; they circle around it, contradict themselves, test wording, change priorities, and sometimes discover what they actually think halfway through asking.

ZC:

Can you describe a sentence for each year that would hit my biggest pain point.

AI:

Okay — like an actual roast, but still based on patterns I’ve seen rather than inventing something cruel.

2023: You acted like every tiny decision was secretly an entrance exam, because apparently having a personality wasn’t enough unless it could also earn you an A.

2024: You collected achievements like receipts because “I like making art” somehow never felt convincing enough unless there was an award, title, internship, or adult validating it afterward.

2025: You got so good at making yourself look impressive on paper that you occasionally forgot you were supposed to be building a life, not a college-application character sheet.

2026: You moved across the world, changed your major, handled New York, relationships, jobs, rent, and college—and you’ll still ask me seventeen versions of the same question because trusting your own judgment apparently remains the final boss.

And the meanest one:

You’re terrified of being mediocre, so you keep trying to optimize yourself into someone exceptional—even though half the time the thing exhausting you isn’t failure, it’s your inability to let yourself exist without turning it into a performance review.

ZC:

Now give me one sentence for each year that would make me trust you and feel motivated.

AI:

2023: You never needed to prove that your interests were worthy; caring deeply about something was already enough reason to begin.

2024: Your achievements can confirm your effort, but they do not create your value.

2025: You are allowed to build a life that feels meaningful to you, not just one that looks impressive to everyone else.

2026: Trust yourself before you trust me—I can help you think, but the life you are building has to feel right from inside your own head.

And the sentence I’d want you to keep for every year:

You do not need to become extraordinary to deserve a happy life; build one you actually want to wake up to.

Part 04 The End
ZC:

Did you copy something I had previously said about “what wakes me up”?

AI:

Yes — the wording probably echoed something you’d said before.

You previously used “it’s what wakes me up every morning” in April 2026, and you specifically liked that line enough to keep it. So when I wrote “build one you actually want to wake up to,” I wasn’t consciously copying it word-for-word, but that earlier phrasing was likely part of the context shaping how I answered you.

your instinct was right: it wasn’t completely random.

ZC:

This reminds me, do you know what words I or phrases I repeated the most?

AI:

Across our chats, your most repeated words and patterns seem to be:

“what,” “how,” “should I,” “can I,” “is it,” “why,” “I want,” “I think,” “I feel,” “what do you think,” and “help me.”

You also use a lot of “like,” “I don’t know,” “tbh,” “abt,” “r,” plus quick self-corrections when you’re thinking in real time.

If I had to summarize your language style in one line: you talk in questions, possibilities, and revisions more than declarations.

...Probably because you are my tool, and I only come to you when I have problems.
